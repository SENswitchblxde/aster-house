Saying "I need an editor" is a bit like saying "I need a doctor." True, probably. But a dermatologist won't do much for your knee.

Editing isn't one job. It's several, done by people who notice very different things. Some will tell you your story starts in the wrong place. Others will tell you there's a double space on page 212. Both are right, and both are annoying in their own special way.

So here's a friendly field guide to the main types of editing, roughly in the order your draft should meet them. To keep things fair, we'll hand each editor the same sentence:

  <p class="specimen__label">The sentence</p>
  <p class="specimen__text">Suddenly, the the dragon, who was very big, suddenly flew away suddenly</p>
</figure>

Let's see what they do with it.

<p class="stage"><span>Stage 1 of 5</span><span>The whole book</span></p>

## Developmental editing: the architect

The developmental editor looks at the whole building before anyone argues about paint colours. Does the story work? Does the argument hold up? Is chapter three doing anything, or just loitering around, taking up space?

This is the big-picture edit. Expect notes like "your villain vanishes for 80 pages" or "your real book starts on page 40." It's usually done on a complete draft, and it can mean serious rewriting.

<figure class="markup">
  <p class="markup__text">Suddenly, the the dragon, who was very big, suddenly <mark>flew away</mark> suddenly</p>
  <figcaption class="markup__note"><span class="markup__who">The developmental editor's note</span>“Why is the dragon leaving? It's the most interesting thing in the book. What if it stayed?”</figcaption>
</figure>

It's possible the whole plot just changed. Yikes.

<p class="needs"><span>You need this when</span>you have a finished draft and a nagging feeling that something's off, but zero clue what it could be.</p>

<p class="stage"><span>Stage 2 of 5</span><span>The order of things</span></p>

## Structural editing: the furniture mover

This is where people get confused. Some editors fold structural editing into developmental editing; others offer it on its own. The simple difference: the developmental editor asks *what* your piece should be. The structural editor asks *what order* it should be in.

They move chapters, merge sections, trim repetition and smooth the path from one part to the next. Think of someone who keeps all your furniture but finally puts the sofa where the sofa belongs.

It's especially handy for non-fiction, reports and anything with a lot of moving parts.

<figure class="markup">
  <p class="markup__text markup__text--move">Suddenly, the the dragon, who was very big, suddenly flew away suddenly <span class="markup__arrow" aria-hidden="true">→ end of chapter</span></p>
  <figcaption class="markup__note"><span class="markup__who">The structural editor's note</span>“Great exit, wrong place. It's buried mid-chapter. Move it to the end so the chapter closes on the dragon leaving.”</figcaption>
</figure>

<p class="needs"><span>You need this when</span>the content is good, but readers keep getting lost.</p>

<p class="stage"><span>Stage 3 of 5</span><span>The sentence</span></p>

## Line editing: the stylist

Now we zoom in. The line editor goes sentence by sentence, tuning rhythm, tone, word choice and clarity. Their job is to make your writing sound like you, on a very good day.

They'll cut your fourth "suddenly," untangle the sentence you wrote at 2 a.m., and gently ask whether every character really needs to "sigh" quite so often. I just did a book with so many "he smiled faintly"s that I wanted to shake the milquetoast character.

<figure class="markup">
  <p class="markup__text"><del>Suddenly, the the dragon, who was very big, suddenly flew away suddenly</del> <ins>The dragon spread its wings and was gone.</ins></p>
  <figcaption class="markup__note"><span class="markup__who">The line editor's note</span>Shorter, cleaner, and nobody had to say “very big.”</figcaption>
</figure>

<p class="needs"><span>You need this when</span>the story is solid, but the writing feels clunky, flat, or a little too fond of adverbs.</p>

<p class="stage"><span>Stage 4 of 5</span><span>The rules</span></p>

## Copyediting: the rule keeper

The copyeditor is the friend who notices your shoelace is untied, and also that you've spelled your main character's name two different ways.

They check grammar, punctuation, spelling and consistency. Is it "email" or "e-mail"? Did the hero's eyes turn from green to brown in chapter nine? Is that a hyphen or an en dash? (Yes, there's a difference. No, they won't stop explaining it.)

Most work from a style guide, so your writing follows the same rules from the first page to the last.

<figure class="markup">
  <p class="markup__text">Suddenly, the <del>the</del> <mark>dragon</mark>, who was very big, suddenly flew away suddenly</p>
  <figcaption class="markup__note"><span class="markup__who">The copyeditor's note</span>“Duplicate ‘the.’ Also, on page 3 it's ‘Dragon’ with a capital D. Pick one.”</figcaption>
</figure>

<p class="needs"><span>You need this when</span>the writing is basically done and you want it clean and consistent.</p>

<p class="stage"><span>Stage 5 of 5</span><span>The full stop</span></p>

## Proofreading: the last line of defence

Proofreading is the final check before your work meets the world. It happens on the near-final version, often the formatted layout, and catches whatever slipped through: typos, missing words, stray spaces, wobbly page numbers.

It's the equivalent of checking your teeth for spinach before a date. Small thing. Big consequences if you skip it.

A proofreader won't rewrite your sentences or question your plot. That ship has sailed, and frankly, so has the dragon.

  <figcaption class="markup__note"><span class="markup__who">The proofreader's note</span>“Missing full stop. Otherwise, lovely.”</figcaption>
</figure>

<p class="needs"><span>You need this when</span>you're about to publish, print or hit send.</p>

## Why the order matters

The golden rule: don't proofread a chapter you might delete. Polishing commas on page 50 is wasted effort if pages 40 to 60 are about to go. Work from the big picture down to the tiny details, and each round makes the next one easier.

You won't always need every type, either. A blog post might only need a copyedit. A novel might need the full tour.
