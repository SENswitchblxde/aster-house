A cover is not a picture attached to the front of a book. It is a piece of functional design that has to work at three completely different sizes, in two completely different media, for two completely different readers.

## The thumbnail

The first place almost every book is now seen is a retail listing, roughly 40mm wide, next to eleven other books. At that size, an image is a smudge and a subtitle is a grey line. What survives is contrast, one strong shape, and the title if it has been set large enough.

This is why so many good covers are type-only. It is not minimalism as an aesthetic position. It is the only thing that reads at 40mm.

## The table

The second is the front table or the shelf face-out, where the reader is three feet away and deciding whether to pick it up. Here the cover has to communicate category before anything else. A reader can tell a literary novel from a thriller from a business book from across a room, and they do it in well under a second, using signals nobody consciously catalogues: typeface, colour temperature, how much of the cover is empty, where the author's name sits, whether there is a rule under the title.

Getting this wrong is not a matter of taste. A memoir dressed as a business book will be picked up by people who do not want it and skipped by people who do.

## The hand

The third is the object itself. Weight, the give of the board, whether the lamination is matt or soft-touch, whether the title is blind debossed so you find it with a thumb before you read it, whether the spine is square and the book opens flat.

This is the part that cannot be judged from a PDF and is the single most common reason a first-time author is disappointed by their finished copies. The file looked right. The object was never specified.

## What we ask before designing anything

- What are the three books this one will sit beside?
- What is the reader expected to already know?
- Is this a book someone buys for themselves or is given?
- Is it a single title or the first of a list?
- Will it be read mostly in print, mostly on a screen, or given as an object?

The answers change everything: the trim size, the extent, the paper, whether there is a jacket, how the title breaks across lines.

## And one thing we do not do

We do not present twelve cover routes. Twelve routes is not generosity, it is a designer declining to have a point of view and asking the author to art-direct by committee. Two or three considered directions, each with an argument behind it, produces a better book and a shorter argument.
