Most people who want to write a memoir stall in the same place. They sit down to begin, and the question that arrives is *where does this start*, and the honest answer is somewhere around 1954 with a grandparent, which is clearly wrong, so they close the file.

The problem is not a lack of material. It is that the material is being asked to arrive in chronological order, which is the least interesting order available and the hardest one to write.

## Start with a room

In interviews, we do not ask people to start at the beginning. We ask them to describe a specific afternoon. Not a year, not a phase — an afternoon, with a room in it, and someone else in the room.

The reason is mechanical. Chronology is stored as summary; scenes are stored as detail. Ask about 1978 and you get a paragraph. Ask about the afternoon the letter arrived and you get the temperature of the room, what your mother was doing with her hands, what was on the radio, and what nobody said at dinner. The second is a book. The first is a CV.

Once four or five of those exist, the shape of the memoir usually declares itself. The scenes have gravity; the connective tissue can be written afterwards.

## A life is not a book

The hardest editorial conversation in memoir is that a complete life makes a poor book and a partial one makes a good one. *Everything that happened to me* has no shape. *The nine years I spent trying to get the business back* has a shape, and everything else in the life can be admitted as evidence when it is needed.

This is not about leaving things out for lack of space. It is that a reader needs to know what the book is about by page twenty, and "a person, from birth" is not a subject.

## The three questions

Before a word is written, we want three answers.

**Who is this for?** A book written for your grandchildren is a different book from one written for people in your industry, and they cannot be the same manuscript with a different cover. Both are legitimate. Choosing one is not optional.

**What is the reader supposed to understand by the end that they did not understand at the start?** In memoir this is rarely a lesson. More often it is a re-reading of something the book showed you early and you misjudged.

**What are you not willing to write?** Every memoir has material the author will not use — other people's privacy, an ongoing legal matter, a wound that has not closed. It is far better to establish this at the outset than to discover it in chapter eleven, after it has been drafted.

## On not writing it yourself

Some people should write their own memoir. Some people have a remarkable life, no interest in prose, and a demanding job — and for them, working with a ghostwriter is not a shortcut, it is the correct tool.

The process is not that someone invents your life. It is that someone conducts twenty to forty hours of structured interviews, reads whatever archive exists, builds a chapter outline you approve, and then does the part that is a craft rather than a memory: making the sentences carry what you meant.

You still make every decision. You just do not have to face the blank page at eleven at night after a full day.
