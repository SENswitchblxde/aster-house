Sooner or later, someone at a dinner says it.

<figure class="specimen">
  <p class="specimen__label">The sentence</p>
  <p class="specimen__text">“You should write a book.”</p>
</figure>

Usually after the second story about the early days. The bank that said no. The supplier who saved you. The year it nearly all went wrong. Everyone nods, someone says it again, and you go home thinking: *should I?*

Maybe. A good business book can do things no brochure, podcast or LinkedIn post can. It can also eat a year of your evenings and end up in boxes in the office storeroom. The difference is rarely the writing. It's whether the book should exist in the first place, and whether you know why.

So here's a straight decision guide: who should write one, who shouldn't, what kind of book it might be, and what it will really cost you in time.

## The good reasons

<p class="stage"><span>Reason 1</span><span>You know something other people need</span></p>

Not your whole life story. One thing, learned the hard way, that would save someone else the trouble. How to hand a family firm to the next generation without a war at the dining table. How to sell to government buyers. Why your first three hires matter more than your first funding round.

If you can name the reader and the problem, you probably have a book.

<p class="stage"><span>Reason 2</span><span>You keep explaining the same thing</span></p>

If you've given the same talk, the same onboarding speech or the same advice over chai forty times, that's a chapter outline in disguise. A book lets you say it properly, once, and hand it over.

<p class="stage"><span>Reason 3</span><span>The story will be lost otherwise</span></p>

This is especially true of family businesses. The founder who started with one shop and a borrowed scooter won't be around forever, and the people who remember the early decisions get fewer every year. A company history or legacy book is often less about readers in bookshops and more about the people who'll be running the business in twenty years.

<p class="stage"><span>Reason 4</span><span>You want to be taken seriously in your field</span></p>

A well-made book still carries weight. It gets you invited to speak, quoted and remembered. But note the words *well-made*. A thin, rushed book does the opposite: it tells everyone exactly how much care went into it.

## The bad reasons

Some reasons sound fine at dinner but won't survive a year of work.

**“It'll be a great business card.”** It might. But if that's the only reason, a good website and a well-written article will do the same job for a fraction of the effort. A book is a very expensive business card.

**“It'll make money.”** Most business books earn their keep indirectly: clients, credibility, speaking, hiring. If you're counting on royalties, count again.

**“Everyone in my industry has one.”** Then yours needs a reason to be different, not just a reason to exist.

**“I'll write it in my spare time.”** You won't have any. More on that below.

**“My life has been interesting.”** It probably has. But interesting to whom? A memoir needs a reader as much as a how-to book does. The question isn't *was it interesting*, but *who is it for, and what will they take away?*

## The one-sentence test

Before anything else (outlines, ghostwriters, cover designs) try to write what the book does in one sentence. Not what it's about. What it does for the reader.

If you can't write that sentence yet, you're not ready to write the book. That's fine. It's the most useful thing to find out early.

## What it costs you in time

This is where most business books quietly die. Not because the idea was bad, but because nobody planned for the hours.

<p class="stage"><span>Route 1</span><span>Writing it yourself</span></p>

Most business books land somewhere between 40,000 and 60,000 words. At a steady 500 words a day, that's three to four months of daily writing for a first draft, before any rewriting, any editing, or any of the weeks when the business needs all of you.

Realistically, a founder writing alone is looking at a year or more. It can absolutely be done. It needs a fixed slot in the diary, the discipline to defend it, and someone (an editor, a trusted colleague) reading chapters as you go, so you don't discover at word 50,000 that the book changed direction at word 12,000.

<p class="needs"><span>This suits you if</span>you enjoy writing, you have a regular window you can protect, and you want every sentence to be yours.</p>

<p class="stage"><span>Route 2</span><span>Working with a ghostwriter</span></p>

A ghostwriter writes the book with you, in your voice, from your ideas and experience. You still put in time, but it's a different kind of time: talking rather than typing.

At Aster House Books, that usually means 15 to 40 hours of recorded interviews, spread over several weeks, plus time to approve the outline and read chapters as they arrive. The research, structure, drafting and editing are on us.

<p class="needs"><span>This suits you if</span>you have the material and the opinions, but not the evenings. Or if you're a great talker and a reluctant writer. (Many founders are.)</p>

Is a ghostwritten book still *your* book? Yes. The ideas, the experience, the judgement and the voice are yours. The ghostwriter's job is to get them onto the page in the right order, and the finished manuscript belongs to you.

<p class="stage"><span>Either way</span><span>The part people forget</span></p>

The manuscript is not the book. After it come editing, cover and interior design, proofreading, ISBN and printing. Give that a few months too, and don't let anyone rush it. A business book gets judged by how it looks on a desk as much as by what's inside.

## Five questions before you start

If you're still deciding, answer these honestly:

1. Can I describe the reader in one sentence?
2. Can I say what they'll get from the book in one sentence?
3. Is there anything here that isn't already in my talks, posts or company deck?
4. Am I comfortable with the difficult parts being in print?
5. Can I commit real time to it, whether that's writing hours or interview hours?

Four or five yeses: you probably have a book. Fewer, and you might have an article, a talk, or a book you'll be ready for in a couple of years. None of those is a failure.
