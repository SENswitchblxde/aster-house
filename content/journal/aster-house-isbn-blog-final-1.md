# Do I Need an ISBN?

## What Authors Need to Know

There is a particular moment in the publishing process when an author discovers the ISBN. Suddenly, publishing feels less like writing a book and more like applying for a passport for it. There are numbers, forms, publishers, formats, barcodes – and, inevitably, someone saying, “You’ll need an ISBN for that.”

So: do you actually need one?

Yes. No. It depends. And, as with most things in publishing, the answer is slightly less straightforward than the question.

## First things first: what is an ISBN?

ISBN stands for International Standard Book Number, a unique number used to identify a particular book or edition of a book. Think of it as an identification number for a specific publishing product.

Your paperback has one, but your hardcover may have another. Your ebook may have another. A revised edition? That may need another too.

The important word here is specific.

An ISBN doesn't identify you as an author. It identifies a particular edition and format of a particular book. So if The Very Serious Memoirs of a Very Tired Woman comes out as a paperback and an ebook, those are two different products. They don't necessarily share an ISBN.

## Do you legally need an ISBN?

Here's where things get confusing.

An ISBN is not the same thing as copyright registration, and having one isn't what makes your book legally a book.

You can write a manuscript without an ISBN. You can copyright a work without an ISBN.

You can even publish in some circumstances without one.

An ISBN is primarily an identification and distribution tool.

That distinction matters because authors sometimes treat the ISBN as the final official stamp that transforms their manuscript into a Published Book™.

It isn't.

It is, however, extremely useful if you intend to sell and distribute the book as a proper publication.

## So why bother?

Because books have to be identifiable. A bookseller, distributor, library or online retailer needs to know exactly which book they're dealing with.

Imagine two editions of the same title:

The Moon Over Delhi
Paperback — 280 pages

and

The Moon Over Delhi
Hardcover — revised edition, 310 pages

The author and title are the same but these are two different products.

The ISBN helps everyone keep them straight. It also makes ordering, cataloguing, inventory management and distribution considerably less chaotic.

## What about Amazon?

This is where authors often get another surprise. Selling your book through an online platform doesn't necessarily mean you personally need to go and obtain an ISBN before you can upload anything.

Different platforms have different systems and requirements. Some provide their own identifiers for particular editions or formats; others allow you to use your own ISBN.

So don't assume: “I'm publishing on Amazon, therefore I need to buy an ISBN.”

Check the requirements of the particular platform and format you're using.

And if you're planning to distribute your book more widely – through bookstores, libraries, distributors or multiple retailers – the question becomes rather more important.

## One book does not always mean one ISBN

This is probably the bit that catches authors out most often.

Suppose you publish a paperback, hardcover, ebook, and an audiobook – so now you have four different publishing products.

They can require separate ISBNs depending on the format and distribution arrangement. Likewise, a substantially revised edition may require a new ISBN.

This is why buying an ISBN isn't necessarily the same thing as having the ISBN situation sorted.

## What about the barcode?

Ah yes. The rectangle of mysterious lines that appears on the back of books.

That's the barcode, not the ISBN itself. The ISBN is the number. The barcode is a machine-readable representation of that number, generally used to make the ISBN scannable at the point of sale. So when someone asks you for the ISBN and you send them a photograph of the barcode, you have technically answered a different question.

This happens more often than you'd think.

## What about India?

For authors publishing in India, ISBNs are issued through the Raja Rammohun Roy National Agency for ISBN, under the Department of Higher Education, Ministry of Education, Government of India.

The application process and eligibility requirements can change, so authors should check the current official guidance rather than relying on something a publishing forum told them in 2019.

## Does an ISBN prove that you're a legitimate publisher?

No, an ISBN does not magically make a book prestigious, or ensure that the manuscript was professionally edited, reviewed or even read by anyone,

It simply means the publication has been assigned an identifier. A beautifully edited book and a terrible book can both have ISBNs.

The ISBN is not judgmental. Thankfully.

## Should an author get their own ISBN?

If you're publishing independently and want control over your publication details and distribution, having your own ISBN can make sense.

But whether you should obtain one yourself depends on how you're publishing, where you're distributing and who is acting as the publisher.

This is one of those occasions where spending five minutes understanding the publishing arrangement can save you a surprising amount of confusion later.

**Ask:**

Who is the publisher of record?

Whose ISBN is being used?

Does that ISBN belong to me, my publishing company, or the platform?

Will I be able to use the same ISBN across distributors?

Do I need separate ISBNs for my different formats?

Those are much better questions than simply asking, “Do I need an ISBN?”

## The short version

If you're just writing a manuscript, no, you don't need an ISBN.

If you're preparing to publish, you may need or strongly benefit from one, depending on your format and distribution plans.

If you're working with a publisher or publishing service, find out whose ISBN is being used and who is listed as the publisher.

And if someone tells you that buying an ISBN is what makes your book officially published ask them to explain what they mean. We’d like to know, too.
